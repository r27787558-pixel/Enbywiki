# Sacred & Classical Traditions

If the ancient Mediterranean shows us gender-variant priests, the wider world shows us something even richer: entire cultural institutions built around people we might now call transgender. Many of these traditions are still alive today.

## Hijra: the third nature of South Asia

The **hijra** communities of India, Pakistan and Bangladesh trace their roots through millennia of South Asian history. Early texts speak of the *tritiya prakriti*, a "third nature," and the *Kama Sutra* discusses people whose desires and genders fall outside the male–female pair.

Hijras held recognised roles in **Mughal courts** as administrators, guards and confidantes, and their blessings — *badhai* — are still sought at births and weddings today. Their status collapsed under British rule, when colonial law reclassified them as a criminal caste (see [Colonialism & Suppression](/docs/colonial-suppression)). In 2014 the Indian Supreme Court recognised a **third gender** in law, and in 2019 the *Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act* followed — imperfect, but a formal reversal of colonial policy.

## Two-Spirit peoples of North America

Before European contact, hundreds of Indigenous nations across what is now the United States and Canada recognised gender roles outside the binary:

- **Nádleehí** among the Diné (Navajo)
- **Lhamana** among the Zuni, where We'wha (1849–1896) became famous as a weaver, potter and cultural ambassador
- **Wíŋkte** among the Lakota
- **Alyha** and **hwame** among the Mojave, who honoured gender change as a sacred calling

In 1990, at a gathering in Winnipeg, the term **Two-Spirit** was adopted by Indigenous LGBTQ+ people as an English umbrella for these culturally specific identities. It is not a synonym for "transgender" or "gay": it belongs to Indigenous communities and refers to a role within a particular nation and spiritual tradition.

## The Pacific

The Pacific Islands developed some of the world's most visible third-gender traditions:

| Culture | Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Samoa | *fa'afafine* | "In the manner of a woman" |
| Hawai'i | *māhū* | A person of dual or intermediate spirit |
| Tonga | *fakaleitī* | Assigned male, living as a woman |
| Aotearoa | *whakawāhine* | Māori term for trans women |

These roles were accepted and sometimes honoured. As with the hijra, colonisation — especially through Christian missionaries and imported anti-sodomy law — brought severe repression, from which communities are still recovering.

## The Americas and beyond

- The **muxe** of the Zapotec people in Oaxaca, Mexico, form a recognised third gender, celebrated each November.
- **Travesti** in Brazil and much of Latin America is a distinct gender identity, not merely a style of dress.
- In early Islamic Medina, *mukhannathūn* were gender-variant performers and servants; some hadiths treat them with tolerance.
- Ottoman *köçek* dancers and Balkan **sworn virgins** show yet more ways societies made room for gender variance.

## The "berdache" problem

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century anthropologists lumped many of these roles together under the pejorative **"berdache,"** a label that erased their differences and treated them as a single curiosity. Reclaiming specific, community-chosen names — Two-Spirit, fa'afafine, hijra — is part of undoing that harm.

These traditions tell a simple truth: the rigid two-gender system is not the human default. It is one arrangement among many, and for much of history it was not the most common one.
