# Medieval to Early Modern

Between the fall of Rome and the Age of Revolutions, European and Islamic societies wrestled with gender in law, religion and everyday life. The evidence is fragmentary — court records, saints' lives, medical texts — but it shows that gender variance never disappeared; it was constantly being argued over.

## Gender in law and medicine

Roman law had already recognised people born with bodies that were neither clearly male nor female. The jurist **Ulpian** (d. 223 CE) wrote that a *hermaphroditus* could be declared male or female, and later canon lawyers debated whether such a person might swear an oath, marry, or inherit.

Medieval **canon law** and its commentators developed the idea that a person whose sex was ambiguous could *choose* a gender, provided the choice was made publicly and consistently. In practice this was about inheritance and marriage, not identity as we understand it — but it is striking evidence that European law once treated sex as something less than absolutely fixed.

Meanwhile, physicians inherited ancient theories of a **spectrum** between male and female, in which bodies were thought to differ by degree, not kind.

## Cross-dressing saints and holy women

Medieval Europe produced a remarkable genre of stories about people assigned female at birth who lived as men — usually to pursue religious life, education, or safety:

- **Saint Marina/Marinus** (5th century), who entered a monastery as a monk and was later slandered and expelled before being vindicated.
- **Saint Euphrosyne of Alexandria**, who lived as "Smaragdus."
- **Saint Pelagius/Pelagia**, a penitent in Jerusalem.
- **Saint Hildegund**, buried in a monastery after living decades as a man.

These are hagiographies, not biographies, and their theology matters: many read them as women escaping the limitations of their sex, rather than as trans men. Yet they preserve something real — the recurrence, over centuries, of a life pattern that only makes sense as gender crossing.

## Case study: Thomas(ine) Hall, 1620

In colonial Virginia, a servant named **Thomas Hall** — also called Thomasine — was said to have dressed sometimes as a man and sometimes as a woman. When neighbours pressed the question, a court ordered Hall to wear **both men's and women's clothing at once**, combining the breeches and the apron.

It is one of the earliest detailed records of a person in the English colonies whose gender was publicly contested. The court's solution — a forced, visible mixture — reveals how threatening ambiguity was to the emerging colonial order.

## The Islamic world

Early Islamic societies knew the **mukhannathūn**, gender-variant entertainers and musicians, and later the **köçek** dancers of the Ottoman Empire. Islamic legal scholarship developed its own debates about *khuntha* — people of ambiguous sex — and how they should inherit and pray.

## A tightening order

From roughly the sixteenth century, European authorities hardened the binary: clothing laws, marriage rules and criminal statutes increasingly punished anyone who crossed gender lines. The famous legend of **Pope Joan** — a woman said to have reigned as pope — was used for centuries as a cautionary tale about hidden gender, likely without any basis in fact.

By the 1700s, the categories that would dominate the next two centuries — the "invert," the "homosexual," the "transvestite" — were beginning to take shape in European thought.
