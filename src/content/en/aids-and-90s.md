# AIDS, Activism & the 1990s

The 1980s brought a catastrophe that reshaped LGBTQ+ communities, and the 1990s brought a hard-won public language for transgender identity. By the end of the decade, trans people had a name, a movement, a flag — and a memorial for their dead.

## The AIDS crisis

HIV and AIDS devastated transgender communities, particularly trans women of colour, sex workers and people living on the street. They were hit twice: by the virus, and by systems that excluded them from care, housing and even from the gay community's own response.

Trans people organised within and alongside groups like **ACT UP**, demanding access to treatment and dignity for the dying. The crisis made plain that trans people's survival depended on their own activism, because no one else was going to provide it.

## A movement finds its name

The word **transgender** had existed since the 1960s (popularised by Virginia Prince), but the 1990s is when it became the umbrella term for a political community — transsexual, cross-dresser, genderqueer and more. Key moments:

- **1990** – The term **Two-Spirit** is adopted at a gathering in Winnipeg (see [Sacred & Classical Traditions](/docs/sacred-traditions)).
- **1993** – The **March on Washington for Lesbian, Gay and Bi Equal Rights** includes trans demands; the trans contingent is enormous.
- **1993** – Minnesota becomes the first US state to prohibit discrimination on the basis of gender identity.
- **1994** – **Transsexual Menace**, co-founded by **Riki Wilchins**, stages direct actions and pushes gender identity into the national LGBTQ+ agenda.
- **1996** – **Leslie Feinberg** publishes *Transgender Warriors*, a people's history of gender variance.

## Changing the diagnosis

For most of the century, trans identity was treated as a psychiatric disorder. The 1990s began a slow, incomplete shift:

| Year | Change |
| --- | --- |
| 1975 | *Transsexualism* appears in the **ICD-9** |
| 1980 | *Transsexualism* added to the **DSM-III** |
| 1987 | Renamed *Gender Identity Disorder* in the **DSM-III-R** |
| 1990 | *Transsexualism* in the **ICD-10** |
| 2013 | **DSM-5** replaces it with *Gender Dysphoria*, dropping "disorder" |
| 2019 | **ICD-11** moves *Gender Incongruence* out of mental disorders entirely |

Advocates called this **depathologisation**. The catch: many health systems still require a diagnosis to pay for care, so trans people must sometimes seek the very label they fought to remove.

## Remembering the dead

In **1998**, **Rita Hester**, a Black trans woman, was murdered in Allston, Massachusetts. Her case received little attention. In response, **Gwendolyn Ann Smith** held a web-based vigil the following year.

That vigil became the **Transgender Day of Remembrance**, now observed every **20 November** around the world — a day to name those lost to anti-trans violence and to insist that they not be forgotten.

## The flag

In **1999**, US Navy veteran **Monica Helms** designed the **Transgender Pride Flag**. Its stripes are light blue for boys, pink for girls, white for those who are transitioning, intersex, or of neutral or undefined gender — and the pattern is symmetric so that, flown either way, it is always correct. It was first flown at Phoenix Pride in **2000** and has since travelled the world.

## A decade of visibility and loss

The 1990s also brought trans stories into public view through tragedy and art: the 1993 murder of **Brandon Teena** in Nebraska and the film *Boys Don't Cry* (1999); **Billy Tipton**, a jazz musician discovered to be a trans man after his death in 1989; **Leslie Feinberg's** novel *Stone Butch Blues* (1993); and the founding of support networks like the **Transgender Law Center** (2002) in the decade's wake.

Trans people entered the twenty-first century with a movement, a vocabulary, and a flag — but also with the knowledge of how much remained to be won.
