# Weimar & the First Institute

Between the end of the First World War and the Nazi seizure of power, Berlin became the most visibly queer city in the world — and the site of the first organised, medical attempt to care for transgender people. It lasted barely fourteen years before it was looted and burned.

## The Institute for Sexual Science

In 1919 **Magnus Hirschfeld** founded the **Institut für Sexualwissenschaft** in Berlin. It was part research centre, part clinic, part advice bureau. It offered:

- Counselling on sexuality and gender, and marriage and contraception advice
- Treatment for sexually transmitted infections
- A library of roughly 20,000 volumes and thousands of photographs
- Support for people seeking legal changes and medical transition

An attached clinic, led with the surgeon **Erwin Gohrbandt**, became the first place where genital reconstruction surgery was performed with the intention of helping a patient live in a different gender.

## Dora Richter

**Dora Richter** (1892–? ) worked as a housemaid at the Institute and is the first known person to undergo what we would now call gender-affirming surgery. She received a series of operations through the 1920s, culminating in **vaginoplasty in 1931**. She lived publicly as a woman in Berlin and was a familiar figure at the Institute.

After the Nazi raid of 1933, Richter's trail vanishes. Her fate is unknown — one of the many trans lives swallowed by the period that followed.

## Lili Elbe

**Lili Elbe** (1882–1931), a Danish painter born Einar Wegener, travelled to Berlin and Dresden for a sequence of surgeries between 1930 and 1931. She documented the process in a memoir published as *Man into Woman* (1933). A final operation proved fatal, and Elbe died in 1931. Her story later inspired the novel and film *The Danish Girl*, though those dramatisations take considerable liberties with her life.

## A brief flowering

Weimar Berlin also produced tools of everyday recognition:

- Berlin police began issuing **"transvestite certificates"** (*Transvestitenschein*), which let a person wear the clothing of another gender in public without arrest.
- **Das 3. Geschlecht** ("The Third Sex"), a magazine for trans readers, ran from 1930 to 1932.
- Bars, balls and clubs made trans people visible in a way that would not be possible again for decades.

This visibility was precarious. It depended on a fragile republic, a handful of clinics, and the willingness of police and doctors to tolerate what much of society did not.

## The burning

On **6 May 1933**, Nazi students and SA members raided the Institute, seized its library and carried it into the Opernplatz to burn it. It was one of the first large book burnings of the Nazi era — and the destruction was deliberate: the Institute's research on sex and gender was exactly what the regime wanted to erase.

Hirschfeld, who was abroad, never returned to Germany. He died in exile in France in 1935, on his birthday. The first chapter of organised transgender medicine ended not in decline but in fire.
