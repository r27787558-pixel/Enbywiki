# Stonewall & Liberation

The modern LGBTQ+ movement is usually dated to a police raid and a riot in New York City in June 1969. That brief uprising was not the beginning of trans resistance — but it became its most famous symbol, and trans women of colour were central to it.

## Before Stonewall

Trans people had already fought back many times:

- In **1959**, drag queens, trans women and gay men clashed with Los Angeles police at Cooper Do-nuts, after police tried to arrest people for "vagrancy."
- In **1966**, the Compton's Cafeteria riot in San Francisco (see [Mid-Century Turbulence](/docs/midcentury)) saw trans women fight police in the street.
- Through the 1960s, street queens and sex workers organised in San Francisco and New York, even as mainstream gay groups tried to distance themselves.

## June 28, 1969

In the early hours of **28 June 1969**, police raided the **Stonewall Inn**, a Mafia-run gay bar in Greenwich Village, ostensibly over liquor licences. This time, the patrons fought back. Crowds grew, windows broke, and the confrontation continued for several nights.

**Marsha P. Johnson** and **Sylvia Rivera**, both trans women of colour, were on the front lines. Rivera later described throwing one of the first Molotov cocktails. The often-repeated claim that Johnson threw "the first brick" has been disputed — including by Johnson herself — but the deeper point stands: trans women were not bystanders at Stonewall, they were leaders.

Within a year, the first **Pride marches** marked the anniversary, and the Gay Liberation Front and later the Gay Activists Alliance organised in the aftermath.

## STAR

In 1970, Johnson and Rivera founded **STAR — Street Transvestite Action Revolutionaries** — one of the first organisations in the world run by and for trans people in the United States. STAR was explicitly for the street queens, homeless youth and sex workers that mainstream groups ignored.

STAR House, on East 2nd Street, gave shelter to homeless trans youth. Rivera and Johnson paid the rent through sex work and scavenging so that younger people would have a roof. The name later evolved toward "Street Transgender Action Revolutionaries" as the word *transgender* took hold.

## "Y'all Better Quiet Down"

In **1973**, Rivera took the stage at the Christopher Street Liberation Day rally and was roundly booed by parts of the crowd. Her response became a defining speech of trans history: *"I will not put up with this shit. I have been beaten. I have had my nose broken. I have been thrown in jail. I have lost my job. I have lost my apartment for gay liberation — and you all treat me this way?"*

The hostility she faced showed that liberation for some was already becoming exclusion for others. Trans people were being written out of the movement they had helped to build.

## Trans activism in the 1970s

The decade brought growing, if fragmented, organising:

- Trans people lobbied medical and psychiatric bodies and published newsletters and journals.
- In **1977**, **Renée Richards**, a trans woman surgeon and tennis player, won the right to compete in the US Open after a landmark court ruling.
- In **1979**, tens of thousands marched in the first **National March on Washington for Lesbian and Gay Rights**, which included trans demands.
- Lesbian feminists such as **Leslie Feinberg** (author of *Stone Butch Blues*, 1993) and activists like **Sandy Stone** pressed the movement to confront gender, not just sexuality.

But the decade also saw the rise of a **trans-exclusionary** current within parts of feminism and the gay movement — most notoriously the 1979 book *The Transsexual Empire* by Janice Raymond. The fights Rivera started in 1973 have never really ended.
