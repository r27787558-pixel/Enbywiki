# Ancient Origins

Transgender and gender-diverse people are not a modern invention. They appear in the very oldest written records we have, long before the words "transgender" or even "homosexual" existed. What changes across history is not whether such people existed, but how their societies named, honoured, punished, or erased them.

> **A note on labels.** We use "transgender" as an umbrella for people whose gender differs from the one assigned to them at birth. Applying it to the distant past is an act of translation, not a claim that ancient people used our vocabulary.

## The first named lives

### Sumer and Mesopotamia

Some of the earliest evidence comes from **Sumer**, where the *gala* were priests and musicians who served the goddess Inanna (later Ishtar). They sang in *eme-sal*, a dialect associated with women, and are described in hymns from around 2000 BCE. Assyrian texts a thousand years later mention the *assinnu* and *kurgarrû* — cultic figures who crossed the lines of gender and were sometimes described as neither man nor woman.

### Ancient Egypt

Egypt's **Hatshepsut** (ruled c. 1479–1458 BCE) governed as a full pharaoh, adopting male titles and regalia. Whether we call this gender identity or political strategy is debated by scholars; either way, it shows that gender presentation was never as rigid as the modern binary implies.

### Greece, Rome and the Galli

In Phrygia and later across the Roman Empire, the **Galli** were priests of the goddess Cybele who lived as women, wore women's clothing, and served her cult. Roman writers viewed them with a mixture of reverence and contempt — a tension that will echo through this whole history.

The emperor **Elagabalus** (r. 218–222 CE) is reported by the historian Cassius Dio to have sought a surgeon who could give him a female body. Because Dio was a hostile source, historians treat the account cautiously — yet it remains one of antiquity's clearest surviving references to what we might now call gender-affirming surgery.

### The Scythian Enarees

Herodotus described the **Enarees**, Scythian diviners he claimed were "womanish men," who wore women's dress and interpreted the future. Their role resembles that of many sacred gender-variant figures worldwide.

## A world of third and fourth genders

Across continents, cultures developed recognised categories beyond "man" and "woman":

| Region | Role or term | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mesopotamia | *gala*, *assinnu* | Temple servants of Inanna/Ishtar |
| Rome / Phrygia | *Galli* | Priests of Cybele |
| Scythia | *Enarees* | Diviners described by Herodotus |
| Ancient India | *tritiya prakriti* | "Third nature," discussed in early texts |

These were not merely private identities. They were structured social roles, often with religious authority, that their communities depended on.

## Why origins matter

It is tempting to begin trans history in the twentieth century, with clinics and diagnoses. But doing so quietly accepts the idea that trans people are a recent phenomenon — a claim used, again and again, to justify erasure. The record says otherwise. Wherever there is writing, there are people whose gender did not fit the mould, and societies that found ways to make room for them.
