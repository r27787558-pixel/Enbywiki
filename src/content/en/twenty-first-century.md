# The Twenty-First Century

The twenty-first century has seen transgender people achieve a level of visibility and legal recognition unimaginable a generation earlier — and, in response, a fierce and coordinated backlash. Both are part of the story.

## Visibility in popular culture

Trans people moved from the margins of media to its centre:

- **Laverne Cox** became the first openly trans person nominated for a Primetime Emmy for acting (*Orange Is the New Black*, 2014).
- **Janet Mock**, author of *Redefining Realness* (2014), became a leading writer and advocate.
- **Chaz Bono** and, in 2015, **Caitlyn Jenner** brought trans lives into mainstream celebrity coverage.
- **Pose** (2018–2021) employed the largest cast of trans actors in a scripted series and centred Black and Latina trans women in 1980s New York.
- In 2020, **Elliot Page** came out as a trans man.

Documentaries, memoirs, and online communities have made trans experience accessible on a scale never seen before.

## Legal breakthroughs

Countries began replacing medical gatekeeping with **self-determination** — the principle that a person's own declaration should be enough to change their legal gender:

| Year | Country / decision | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2012 | Argentina | Gender Identity Law: self-determination, no diagnosis |
| 2014 | Denmark | Dropped surgery and sterilisation requirements |
| 2015 | Ireland, Malta | Legal gender recognition on the basis of self-declaration |
| 2018 | Chile, Portugal | Strengthened self-determination laws |
| 2020 | **Bostock v. Clayton County** (US Supreme Court) | Anti-discrimination law protects trans workers |
| 2023 | Spain | "Trans law" broadens self-determination |
| 2024 | Germany | Self-Determination Act replaces the old expert-panel system |

The **Yogyakarta Principles** (2007) and **Yogyakarta Principles plus 10** (2017) set out how existing human-rights law applies to gender identity and sexual orientation.

## Health and standards of care

- **WPATH** published its *Standards of Care* version 7 in 2012 and version 8 in 2022, moving toward more individualised, less gatekept care.
- **ICD-11**, adopted in 2019 and in force from 2022, removed gender incongruence from the chapter on mental disorders.
- **Informed-consent** models spread, allowing adults to access hormone therapy without psychiatric evaluation.

None of this is universal: in many countries, trans people still need a judge's or a panel's permission to be recognised.

## The backlash

Greater visibility provoked organised reaction. In the United States, state legislatures passed hundreds of bills targeting trans people — restricting bathrooms, school sports, and gender-affirming care, with several states banning care for minors. In the United Kingdom, reform of the Gender Recognition Act stalled amid hostile media coverage, and waiting lists for gender clinics stretched to years. Similar movements emerged across Europe and beyond.

This backlash is not new in kind — it borrows directly from the colonial and medical histories described earlier. What is new is its coordination and its focus on children.

## Resistance, joy and care

Trans communities responded with mutual aid, political organising and culture:

- The **Transgender Day of Visibility** (founded 2009 by Rachel Crandall) balances mourning with celebration.
- **Trans Lifeline** (2014) runs peer support and a bail fund.
- Grassroots groups fund hormones, surgeries, and legal fees for those cut off from care.
- Artists, athletes, scientists and everyday people continue to live openly, making the future imaginable for those who come next.

## Where next

The unfinished work is clear: universal self-determination, access to healthcare, safety from violence, and an end to the criminalisation that empire began. History suggests that progress is neither linear nor guaranteed — but also that trans people have outlasted every attempt to erase them.
