# Colonialism & Suppression

European empires did not merely encounter gender-variant traditions around the world — they dismantled them. Colonial law, medicine and religion worked together to impose a binary of man and woman, backed by police power, and to criminalise everyone who fell outside it. Much of the transphobia that persists today is a colonial inheritance.

## Criminalising gender in South Asia

The British East India Company and later the Crown rewrote Indian law around the binary:

- **Section 377** of the Indian Penal Code (1861) criminalised "carnal intercourse against the order of nature," a law that survived until 2018 and was exported to dozens of British colonies.
- The **Criminal Tribes Act of 1871** classified hijra communities as a hereditary "criminal tribe," subjecting them to registration, surveillance and arrest. The Act was repealed in 1952, but the stigma it manufactured outlived it.

Before colonisation, hijras had held courtly and religious roles. After it, they were pushed to the margins, forced into begging and sex work — a pattern repeated wherever empire met a third-gender tradition.

## Suppressing Two-Spirit life in North America

In the lands that became the United States and Canada:

- The **Indian Act (1876)** and its amendments, alongside the reservation and pass systems, broke up kinship structures that had supported Two-Spirit people.
- **Residential and boarding schools** punished Indigenous languages, ceremonies and gender roles together, an explicit project of cultural erasure.
- Anthropologists labelled these roles "berdache," treating them as a disorder to be catalogued rather than a tradition to be understood.

The word **Two-Spirit**, adopted in 1990, is in part a direct response to this history: a term chosen by the communities themselves.

## The Pacific and Africa under mission and law

Christian missionaries across the Pacific condemned *fa'afafine*, *māhū* and related roles as sin, while imported anti-sodomy statutes made them illegal. In much of Africa, colonial legal codes similarly recast pre-existing gender and sexual diversity as "unnatural." Many of those laws remain on the books today and are used to justify ongoing persecution.

## Europe's own purges

Colonial violence abroad rested on growing repression at home. Early modern Europe executed people for sodomy and for cross-dressing; the last known execution for sodomy in Western Europe took place in the 18th century. Sex-segregated institutions, dress codes and marriage law all narrowed what a body was allowed to be.

## Persistence and resistance

The crucial fact is that none of this succeeded in eliminating the people it targeted. Hijra communities reorganised into *jamaats*; Two-Spirit people reclaimed their roles in the twentieth century; Pacific *fa'afafine* became visible advocates for law reform. Suppression shaped trans history profoundly, but it did not end it.
