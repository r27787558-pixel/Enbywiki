# Mid-Century Turbulence

The decades between the Nazi book burnings and the Stonewall uprising saw transgender people scatter, survive, and slowly build new networks. Medicine reseeded its clinics across Europe and North America — often without the human insight Hirschfeld had brought — while a handful of trans people began to organise on their own terms.

## After the ashes

The war destroyed the Institute in Berlin and scattered its researchers across Europe and the United States. Research on gender continued in new hands, but the balance shifted: treatment became more available and, at the same time, more tightly controlled by psychiatrists who saw themselves as gatekeepers rather than advocates.

## Christine Jorgensen

In 1952 a young American ex-serviceman travelled to Copenhagen and returned as **Christine Jorgensen** (1926–1989). She had undergone surgery with the endocrinologist **Christian Hamburger**, and her story broke worldwide under headlines like "Ex-GI Becomes Blonde Beauty."

Jorgensen's openness made her the most famous trans woman of the century. She became an actress and lecturer, and though the press often treated her as a curiosity, she repeatedly insisted on her ordinary dignity. Her visibility made thousands of people realise that transition was possible — and that they were not alone.

## Building networks and institutions

Trans people of the 1950s and 60s could not yet rely on a movement, so they built one piece at a time:

- **Louise Lawrence** (1912–1976), a trans woman in San Francisco, ran a vast correspondence network from her home, connecting isolated people to doctors, information and each other.
- **Virginia Prince** (1912–2009) published *Transvestia* magazine from 1960, creating a community for cross-dressers and trans readers that reached thousands.
- **Reed Erickson** (1917–1992), a wealthy trans man, created the **Erickson Educational Foundation** in 1964, funding research, clinics, and educational materials — arguably the first major private funder of trans health.
- **Harry Benjamin** (1885–1986), an endocrinologist, cared for trans patients for decades and published *The Transsexual Phenomenon* in 1966. The **Harry Benjamin International Gender Dysphoria Association** — today WPATH — was named for him.

The first American university gender clinic opened at **Johns Hopkins** in 1966. It was run by clinicians including **John Money**, whose theories of "gender identity" were influential — and whose coercive practices, especially on intersex infants, later became notorious.

## Compton's Cafeteria, 1966

In August 1966, in San Francisco's Tenderloin district, transgender women and drag queens fought back when police tried to arrest them at **Gene Compton's Cafeteria**. The riot spilled into the street and predates Stonewall by three years. A group called **Vanguard** had been organising in the neighbourhood, and a **Transsexual Counseling Service** followed.

Compton's is often called the forgotten Stonewall. Its story shows that trans resistance in the 1960s was already organised, political, and centred on the poorest members of the community — sex workers, runaways and street youth.

## A hostile mainstream

The same decades brought deepening medical gatekeeping. Would-be patients were evaluated as mentally ill, required to conform to rigid standards of femininity or masculinity, and often denied care. The language of "real" versus "fake" trans people hardened, and the "transsexual" was placed under psychiatric authority for the rest of the century.

Then, in 1969, police raided a bar in Greenwich Village — and something different happened.
