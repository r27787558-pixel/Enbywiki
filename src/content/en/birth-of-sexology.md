# The Birth of Sexology

In the second half of the nineteenth century, a new science of sex emerged in Europe. For the first time, doctors and writers tried to describe people who crossed gender lines using something other than sin or crime. The results were double-edged: sexology gave transgender and same-sex-desiring people a language — and, at the same time, a diagnosis.

## Karl Heinrich Ulrichs and the "urning"

The German lawyer **Karl Heinrich Ulrichs** (1825–1895) is often called the first person to come out publicly for gay rights. Writing under a pseudonym from 1864, he argued that some people were born with a *female soul in a male body* — his Latin phrase was *anima muliebris virili corpore inclusa*.

Ulrichs called himself an **Urning** and described a spectrum that included *Urninge* (attracted to men), *Urninden* (attracted to women) and *Zwitter* (a "hermaphrodite" of the soul). In 1867 he addressed the Congress of German Jurists in Munich, urging the repeal of anti-sodomy laws — reportedly the first public speech for homosexual rights. He was shouted down.

## Naming the "homosexual"

In 1869 the Hungarian writer **Karl-Maria Kertbeny** coined the word *homosexual* in a pamphlet against Prussia's sodomy statute. The word, and the medical framework around it, spread rapidly — reframing desire as an identity rather than an act.

## Magnus Hirschfeld and the word "transvestite"

**Magnus Hirschfeld** (1868–1935), a Jewish-German physician and sexologist, is the pivotal figure of this era. In 1897 he co-founded the **Scientific-Humanitarian Committee** (Wissenschaftlich-humanitäres Komitee), generally considered the world's first gay-rights organisation. It campaigned for years to repeal Paragraph 175, the German law criminalising sex between men.

In 1910 Hirschfeld published *Die Transvestiten* ("The Transvestites"), the book that introduced the term **transvestite**. Crucially, he distinguished cross-gender identification and dress from same-sex desire — an early attempt to separate what we would now call gender identity from sexual orientation. He also argued that many of his patients were neither sick nor criminal, and deserved medical care and legal protection rather than punishment.

## Havelock Ellis and "Eonism"

The British physician **Havelock Ellis** popularised sexology in English. His *Sexual Inversion* (1897) treated same-sex desire as congenital rather than depraved. In *Eonism and Other Supplementary Studies* (1928) he described cross-gender identification under the name **Eonism**, after the Chevalier d'Éon, a French diplomat who lived decades as a woman in the 1700s.

## The double edge of diagnosis

Sexology's legacy is genuinely mixed:

| Gave | Took |
| --- | --- |
| Words for feelings people had always had | Framed trans identity as a disorder |
| A defence against criminal punishment | Placed gatekeeping power in doctors' hands |
| Public debate and visibility | Reinforced the idea of a "normal" sex and gender |

Hirschfeld's answer was institutional: in 1919 he opened the Institute for Sexual Science in Berlin, where the first organised gender-affirming care would be attempted — and then destroyed. That is the subject of [Weimar & the First Institute](/docs/weimar-institute).

> The words of the sexologists were imperfect and often harmful. But they were also the raw material that trans people would later take up, argue with, and remake into their own.
